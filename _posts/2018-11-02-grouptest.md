A common laboratory practice is to pool specimens and test them as a group. For diseases with low prevalence, such as HIV or hepatitis C, a negative result for the group typically indicates that all samples within it are negative, assuming the test has high specificity and sensitivity, which is usually the case.

For example, consider a blood donation center screening 200 samples for HIV. The most straightforward approach is to test each sample individually, requiring 200 tests. In contrast, group testing could divide the 200 samples into 20 groups of 10 samples each. Initially, only 20 tests are needed—one per group. If all groups test negative, no further testing is required. If any group tests positive, additional tests can be conducted, either by splitting the group into smaller subgroups or testing each sample individually. Given the low probability of positive samples, group testing typically requires fewer tests than individual testing.

**What’s the benefit?** Reduced costs and faster results.

A key consideration in group testing is determining the optimal number of groups and their sizes. Dr. Christopher Bilder has conducted extensive research in this area, including identifying optimal group configurations.
